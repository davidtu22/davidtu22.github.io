September 2026 · watching: Unlearning Economics, “Everything Was Already A.I”

Forever Games, LLMs, and the Economy

With the release of OpenAI's Astra model, we're already seeing some trends pop up. From the true-believer crowd, you have examples like uncanny AI replications of popular games — Call of Duty, Fall Guys. You also have the next stage of MMORPG plug-in tools that farm materials while the game runs in the background: WoW and Minecraft are two I've already seen. I struggle to see the use value in these cases next to the more creative ventures shown by games like Infinite Craft. What's the bar, if LLMs are simply being used to create inferior copies of games that already exist — or worse, to play the kinds of games that players would rather have a bot controlling for them?

The crux of the argument lies in a thesis put forward by Dr. Cahal Moran, a behavioral economist who runs the YouTube channel Unlearning Economics — video-essay-style commentary. One of his latest uploads, “Everything Was Already A.I,” argues there's a through-line traceable from media and entertainment to enfranchisement to the current AI boom. Even in the years before AI, there was a noticeable drop in quality across the cultural productions that defined the 2000s and 2010s. A returning example could make you swear this was a video about The Simpsons (Dr. Moran insists it's not): a beloved franchise iconic for quippy, culturally aware humor — how many predictions has The Simpsons gotten right at this point? — that eventually dragged its seasons out, becoming the very thing it once poked fun at, collaborating across its 20th Century Fox IPs with shows it had heavily inspired, like Family Guy and American Dad. Now, with a new movie lined up for next year, it's a case study in Hollywood's bloating problem. You can see the same issue elsewhere. Why do some movies and games released in the last three years look worse than productions from decades ago? Dr. Moran compares the hype around the trailers for Avengers: Infinity War to the reception of Ant-Man and the Wasp: Quantumania, a film that came out during what was perceived as a lull for Marvel Studios. Art direction and cinematography were never Marvel's strongest selling point, but the back-to-back contrast between scenes from both movies reveals just how low the bar had gotten.

Games make maybe an even stronger case. There's a parallel story in the older Batman games — the latest being Arkham Knight, all the way back in 2015 — against the poor reception of their spiritual successors, Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League and Gotham Knights. With the continued rollout of Nvidia's latest AI-assisted DLSS update, there's another disconnect: between product and marketing, and between tech and creative. Graphic fidelity is an interesting pursuit with diminishing returns over time. At some point, how good a game looked stopped mattering as much as its intended art direction — which is very apparent in games specifically. The latest footage of Rockstar's long-awaited GTA VI shows that after a decade, graphics can improve, but what really holds players' attention is the cinematic look and feel of the game. Lucia, Jason, and the rest of the cast look fantastic, but it was never the artists' intention to make these characters photorealistic — a problem DLSS consistently misreads. The trademark AI-filter sheen doesn't work on most games, because that was never how most games were supposed to look. Gamescom 2026, meanwhile, revealed that when all else fails, the safest bet any developer can make is to copy elements of existing games one-to-one — down to specific action sequences and trademark mechanics — package it into a glittery, indiscernible style, and give it a free-to-play, live-service model rife with microtransactions. These are the literal “forever games” that, extrapolated, become a meta-commentary on the other, economic “forever game.”

The monetization problem runs deeper still. Because the only rule every sector follows, at the end of the day, is “line go up,” Dr. Moran cites a study describing the present economy as a scatter of “accountability sinks.” At some point, companies would rather settle for permanent productive and creative stagnation, because they're simply too big to fail.

“Everybody in the industry keeps playing the music even as the bubble gets worse and that everybody knows is a bubble”

— Dr. Cahal Moran, on 2008 and now alike

As Dr. Moran sees it, the economist and computer scientist Herbert Simon — best known for his theories of “bounded rationality” and “satisficing” — basically solved economics a long time ago. The rest, as Moran lays it out, is simply politics. Which makes AI reflective of the larger economic condition.

“Rather than being intelligent agents, Large Language Models combine the features of cultural and social technologies in a new way. They generate summaries of unmanageably large and complex bodies of human-generated information. But these systems do not merely summarize this information, like library catalogs, Internet search, and Wikipedia. They also can reorganize and reconstruct representations or ‘simulations’ of this information at scale and in novel ways, like markets, states, and bureaucracies… Just as market prices are lossy representations of the underlying allocations and uses of resources, and government statistics and bureaucratic categories imperfectly represent the characteristics of underlying populations, so too Large Models are ‘lossy JPEGs’ of the data corpora on which they have been trained.”

— Henry Farrell

To put it another way: LLMs are seamless feedback machines that receive and reciprocate. Later in the video, Farrell continues his inspection of AI's role in a K-shaped economy:

“Ensuring the continued production of high quality knowledge is hard. It is hard when big monopolies or wannabe monopolies have every incentive to batten on the knowledge production systems that they rely on, cannibalizing expensive-and-laborious systems for producing knowledge by flooding the market with cheaper summarizations. Social movements, activists, policy makers should push back against arguments that are, in the long run, both specious and self-undermining. Any division of rights and proceeds ought surely recognize that LLMs are valuable engines of knowledge, but only in conjunction with high quality knowledge in the first place. Enough of the proceeds need to go to the actual knowledge producers for the system to not be self-cannibalizing.”

— Henry Farrell
September 2026 · building: a static Infinite Craft clone

What “Runs on an LLM” Actually Costs

Started as a first web project — a small clone of Neal Agarwal's Infinite Craft, the game where you drag elements together and they combine into new ones. I got it working end to end: drag-and-drop, a recipe table, persistence, the whole loop. Then I got curious about how the real thing actually generates its combinations, and that turned out to be the more interesting project.

Infinite Craft doesn't hard-code any of it. Every new pairing anyone has ever tried gets sent live to an LLM (Llama 2, served through Together.ai), and the result is cached forever after so no one else ever pays for that same combination again. Agarwal has said managing that cost was the hardest part of building the game — and it's easy to see why once you sit with the economics: a game made with an LLM does that work once, at build time, and ships as ordinary static assets from then on. A game that runs on one keeps a live meter running for as long as it exists, and that meter scales with how popular the game gets, not with how much work you put into it. AI Dungeon is the cautionary tale here — Latitude switched off OpenAI's model in 2021 over rising costs and content-moderation pressure, and players felt the resulting quality drop immediately.

Once I understood that distinction, I didn't actually want to run the second kind of game — not as a hobby project with no revenue model, footing an open-ended API bill for whoever finds the link. So the version I deployed stays entirely in the first category: a hand-authored recipe table, procedural in the plain, old-fashioned sense, with zero marginal cost per play. I did build and test a small proof-of-concept backend (Claude Haiku 4.5 generating genuinely novel combinations on request) to see the real architecture work end to end — but it was never deployed for open play, on purpose. The gap between those two versions is basically the whole essay.

September 2026 · reading: Žižek, “Why We Remain Alive Also in a Dead Internet”

A.I. and the Protestant Work Ethic, with a Little Help from Žižek

A cross-examination of generative AI and my capstone project, with a little help from Žižek. A thesis from my capstone was that the political Left reads as socially conservative due to its internalization of the Protestant Work Ethic. A new question surfaces: where does AI lie in this equation?

Take the case of Larian Studios, the developers behind Baldur's Gate 3, Game of the Year 2023 and one of my all-time favorites. Swen Vincke, in an industry interview, casually mentioned that artists working on BG3 used some generative AI for early concept work and brainstorming. This set off a storm of comments, outraged that a studio which had presented itself as anti-AI would do such a thing — which also lends credence to the continued electoral hostility toward AI (Silicon Valley being, culturally, very insulated). Vincke seemed genuinely confused by the pushback. He explained that the decision hadn't come down from above — it was made autonomously by the artists — and that none of the AI-generated art made it into the final release, nor was that ever the intention.

This presents an interesting dialectic against the topics in my capstone. On one hand, the relationship between the future of labor and AI isn't exactly transparent. On the surface, it's marketed as a way to cut down on menial, tedious labor — but I don't know if we're far enough into capitalism for people to seriously entertain some form of luxury automated egalitarianism, let alone its endpoint of fully automated luxury communism. Still, it's a possibility that the largely humanist Left (which I cover in my paper) tends to ignore or actively resist.

On the other hand, any pessimistic Marxist economist would reach for the theory of relative surplus value: automation only reduces socially necessary labor time — the portion of the workday needed to reproduce a worker's wage — not the workday itself. People stay at work just as long; only the ratio of paid-for time to value-generating time shifts. It happened with the industrial revolution, it happened with the internet, and it will keep happening now.

A conversation with an indie developer shed some more light on this. He told me BG3 ran into a strange problem with the rigs of its hundreds of unique characters and NPCs — how cutscenes and animations held up (or didn't) once players started testing the limits of what should have been game-breaking strategies (see: Can You Beat BG3 as a Group of Cats?). AI was part of the solution there. And he told me something true about the industry along with it: player feedback always has to be taken seriously, even when players are — correctly, in this case — totally wrong about how something actually works under the hood, for lack of developer-level knowledge. Larian had built a reputation as one of the most “power to the players” studios out there, and it still only took one offhand comment for the whole industry to quietly agree to a kind of rule of silence. It wasn't worth the effort to explain the specifics anymore, even for a generous, well-respected studio that had spent years earning that reputation. Something similar happened with Sandfall Interactive, I think, before their own vow of silence set in.

In truth, I'm still processing what the end goal is here. Can we strike a balance between humanist and anti-humanist thought about AI? Maybe Slavoj Žižek can help. In my Social Life of Data class, I brought him up twice, using the same Substack piece — both papers are linked below. What's stuck with me since is how Žižek plays with Lacan's idea of the maternal plane: the possibility that even in AI-ruled fictional dystopias, there's an inclination toward habits that are uniquely biological, uniquely human. It's why he titles the piece, fittingly, “Why We Remain Alive Also in a Dead Internet.”

September 2026 · senior colloquium, CSS 391

An Anti-Humanist Case for Peace, with Some Help from Nietzsche

My senior colloquium paper started as a straightforward complaint about the humanist tradition of nonviolence and ended up somewhere much stranger: an argument that the most honest path to peace might run through indifference, not goodwill.

The setup is the Italian Marxist historian Domenico Losurdo's case against Christian abolitionism. Take Charles Stearns, a 19th-century nonviolence proponent who spent three months talking himself out of his own principles — from “it is always practicable to be a non-resistant” to, on the subject of pro-slavery Missourians, “these men are not men,” and therefore fair game. The hypocrisy is obvious, but Losurdo never quite explains why it happens. Nietzsche does: it's slave morality, the move where a group defines “good” as “whatever isn't the enemy” and then treats that definition as self-evident rather than invented. Stearns wasn't failing to live up to nonviolence — he was doing exactly what slave morality does, which is decide the outcome first and back into the justification.

Nietzsche's own image for this is birds of prey and lambs: the lambs call the birds evil for eating them, and there's nothing factually wrong with that, except that it gives the birds no way to not be evil — the verdict was fixed before the hunt. Society, Nietzsche argues, has been stuck seeing the world from the lambs' side ever since, unable to imagine strength that isn't also cruelty. Which is part of why a Nietzschean cult attached itself so easily to fascism — the historian Ishay Landa's read, which I find convincing, is that this wasn't nostalgia for ancient violence. It was a reaction against something new: modernity had made a warless world look achievable for the first time, and that possibility is what fascism actually organized against.

The strongest humanist counter-case is obvious — Martin Luther King. But the more interesting rebuttal, I think, comes from inside the civil rights movement's own left flank, from the Afro-pessimist tradition and specifically Ralph Ellison's “The World and the Jug,” his response to the critic Irving Howe. Howe's complaint was that Ellison and Baldwin weren't angry enough — too busy being writers to be properly militant. Ellison's rebuttal is a hunting story: he and a friend, trespassing on a farmer's land during the 1937 recession, get threatened at gunpoint — until the farmer clocks that they're armed too. Nobody apologizes. Nobody has a change of heart. The standoff just ends, because both sides now know what happens if it doesn't.

“Situations can be more loaded than guns and gestures more eloquent than words.”

— Ralph Ellison, “The World and the Jug”

That's the whole anti-humanist argument in miniature: the peace that held wasn't goodwill, it was a mutual read of the stakes. Nietzsche's Genealogy makes the same point more abstractly — there is no “being” behind the doing, no self hovering above the deed to make it moral or not; the deed is everything. Violence and its absence aren't moral facts. They're just what happens given the actual balance of power in the room.

So what's the anti-humanist alternative, if not “more violence”? Foucault's answer, more or less, is indifference — stepping outside the demand that every political actor perform good faith. It's a strange thing to build a peace politics on. But it's also, I'd argue, more honest than diplomacy's current model, which asks everyone to pretend contempt isn't in the room.

The paper's actual proposal is where it gets genuinely strange: trade. Georg Simmel's Philosophy of Money argues money is the one social relation that doesn't hide behind a face — it's indifferent by design, which is exactly the quality that makes it useful here. Whatever people think of Putin, nobody's found a way to test whether sanctions provoked the war they were meant to prevent. Meanwhile, the reason China hasn't invaded Taiwan probably has less to do with restraint than with the fact that China is bound to the rest of the world's supply chains too tightly to risk it. Not exactly inspiring. But if the anti-humanist critique holds — if “good faith” diplomacy is mostly theater — an indifferent mechanism might do more actual work than a sincere one.

It's an odd note to end a paper about peace on: not love, not justice, just enough interdependence that nobody can afford to want war badly enough. I don't know if I believe it all the way. But it's the first time an argument about Nietzsche has made me think about foreign policy instead of just Nietzsche.

September 2026 · coursework: Guide to Fascism, Theories of Fascism, Kafka

Fascism Lives in the Boring Parts

My final paper for Guide to Fascism opens with a confession: I came in thinking fascism was mostly an ideology — political events, historical trends, a rogues' gallery of famous faces, something to be observed “like a message in a bottle.” By the end I'd changed my mind about where it actually lives. Less in a program than in an intuition; less in the state than in the individual; and the violence I kept running into wasn't the sweeping, eliminationist kind but something stranger — born of indifference, apathy, and a hatred of society at large that doesn't have a name attached to it.

That comes with a caveat I owe to Žižek, whose interview “Everything Provokes Fascism” is the best argument I've read against calling everything fascism. His point is that treating fascism as a guessing game — scanning for insignias, shouting the word at surface phenomena — is exactly how the Left fails to see it. Fascism, for him, is closer to “capitalism without capitalism”: the basic relations preserved, the individualism and disequilibrium stripped out. And his sharpest line is a tragic one: the more the Left attacks people caught in fascism with abstract enlightenment arguments, “the more it gives them arguments to be fascist.” Everything provokes fascism. That is not the same as everything being fascism.

So where does it live? The reading list for Theories of Fascism kept giving the same answer from different angles: not mainly in doctrine, but in desire. Fromm's argument is that authority can't run on coercion alone — a regime that ruled only by fear would paralyze the very work it depends on — so it needs a psychological hook, and he finds it in the petty-bourgeois family, where a dissatisfied father pins his ambitions on his son. The authoritarian character that results is defined by “rebellion against a father coupled with extreme obedience and submission to a leader.” Guattari and Toscano push it further, quoting Reich: the masses “were not deceived; at a particular time, they actually wanted a fascist regime.” That is a much less comfortable claim than “they were tricked,” and I closed that response paper by asking my classmates what elements of fascism they find most desirable. It's an uncomfortable question, and I meant it as one.

Which is where Kafka comes in, and where the argument stops being about psychology and starts being about boredom. Tony Judt credits Kafka with intuiting the rot that interwar Europe was sitting on — “embarrassingly penetrating,” he calls it. What struck me rereading my Kafka paper is how undramatic that rot is. Georg's business in “The Judgment” is stagnating; his one link to the outside world is a Sunday letter about insignificant events; and the story ends not on his death but on a sentence about the world carrying on: “At that moment, a positively endless stream of traffic was going over the bridge.” In “The Metamorphosis,” Gregor's family goes from stunned to annoyed almost immediately, and my argument was that the transformation barely matters — they'd have treated him the same way had he simply become an invalid. Kafka's transformations, I wrote, are a feint: everything appears to change and nothing moves. That stasis is the actual setting. A family that works as an extension of a system of exploitation, an economy that alienates the worker who feeds it, and a world too busy to look up.

Once you see it that way, the Japan material reads differently. I'd already put Sōseki next to Kafka in that paper — two writers whose warnings predate anything you could call explicit fascism. In Sanshirō, a professor tells a young man that Japan will perish and, more usefully, that “even bigger than Japan is the inside of your head. Don't ever surrender yourself — not to Japan, not to anything.” Decades later, the narrator of Nosaka Akiyuki's “American Hijiki” notes that three months after helping the army shoot at Americans he was cleaning up to receive them as guests, now paid in money instead of bread. Nobody is asking these men what they believe; they're being told what to feel about each upheaval and then handed a wage. In Anne Allison's Precarious Japan, the same vacancy shows up in the furita, the gig workers with no stable footing: Akagi Tomohiro, thirty-one, who wrote an essay called “Hope Is War” arguing that war was the only chance for people like him who have nothing; and Kato Tomohiro, who killed seven people in Akihabara after writing, “I've come to hate society and am tired of life. Anyone is OK.” No party, no program, no particular enemy — just a social nihilism looking for somewhere to go.

The same pattern shows up at the level of policy. My Reading Synthesis on David Harvey called neoliberalism's alienated, commodified vulnerability the place where fascism festers; C.G. Tamás's version of the argument, in “Post-Fascism,” is that it no longer needs the stormtroopers at all:

“Post-fascism does not need to put non-citizens into freight trains to take them into death; instead, it need only prevent the new non-citizens from boarding any trains that might take them into the happy world of overflowing rubbish bins that could feed them.”

— C.G. Tamás, “Post-Fascism”

I don't want this to slide into “everything is fascism” — Žižek would be right to yell at me. The claim is narrower and, I think, more useful: if fascism grows mostly in stagnant, alienating, indifferent conditions, then the flags and insignia are the least interesting part of it. The interesting part is the bureaucracy that only has to look away, the wage that arrives with no reason attached, the Sunday letter about nothing. That's a harder thing to fight than a symbol, because most of it looks like an ordinary Tuesday.

September 2026 · coursework: Frankfurt School Critical Theory, Theories of Fascism

Adorno Already Had a Word for Forever Games

In Forever Games, LLMs, and the Economy I leaned on Cahal Moran's argument that everything was already AI — that the flattening of culture into franchises and live-service loops predates any model that could generate it. Reading that entry next to my Culture Industry response paper from Theories of Fascism, it's a little humbling how much of one is already in the other. Horkheimer and Adorno made the argument in the 1940s.

Their first observation is monotony: “culture today is infecting everything with sameness.” Their second is subtler, and it describes nearly every trailer I've watched this year — the culture industry's relationship to novelty is to exclude it while never shutting up about it. “What is new in the phase of mass culture compared to that of late liberalism is the exclusion of the new… That is why there is incessant talk of ideas, novelty and surprises, of what is both totally familiar and has never existed before.” That is, more or less, a live-service game announcement. It's also what Moran means when he says Marvel got worse before AI arrived to take the blame.

They also explain why it keeps happening, and it isn't taste. The culture monopolies ride the coattails of steel, oil, and energy, and the sameness is a product of consolidation — fifty years ago maybe a hundred media companies, news outlets, and banks; now, by my count in the paper, five, trading the same properties back and forth under different logos. And the system absorbs its critics. Parasite winning at the Oscars feels like a progressive victory, but “something is provided for everyone so that no one can escape,” and we leave a little more content with the status quo.

The Dialectic of Enlightenment is the bigger frame, and it's where the LLM material lands. Their claim is that enlightenment, in trying to free us from myth, entangles itself more deeply in it — and that once knowledge is treated the way it treats nature, as something to be mastered, theory becomes “simply a means to an end.” Technology, in their telling, is as democratic as the economy it grew up in: it serves without bias, and produces only methods and mere instruments. Henry Farrell's line that LLMs are “lossy JPEGs” of the knowledge they were trained on, and that the money has to flow back to actual knowledge producers or the system cannibalizes itself, is the same worry in an engineering vocabulary. So is the DLSS section of my Forever Games entry: fidelity is what you can measure, art direction is what you can't, and a system that declares the unmeasurable an illusion — the “equivalence” principle Horkheimer and Adorno describe — will optimize the first at the expense of the second.

Marcuse supplies the piece the first two don't: why a flood of content doesn't feel like a cage. His term is repressive tolerance — tolerance that has gone passive, extended to everything equally, sense and nonsense alike, until the neutrality itself does the work of the status quo. In my paper the example was vaccine misinformation: the argument that someone has a right to be heard assumes their opinion came from earnest inquiry, when it may be what a partisan pundit's algorithm fed them — in which case, I wrote, it wasn't “free” to begin with. The same objection applies to infinite content: unlimited choice from an assortment somebody else assembled.

Habermas's complaint about his teachers is that fascism shook them so badly they concluded everything was deception, which leaves a theory with no way to describe a non-deceptive thing — a performative contradiction. It's a fair objection, and it applies to me here: “everything was already the culture industry” is exactly the kind of sentence that can't lose. I still can't decide whether forever games are the culture industry finishing the job or just a bad decade for a lot of studios. I lean toward the first. I'm suspicious of how satisfying that is.

September 2026 · coursework: Foundations of Critical Theory (Marx, Nietzsche, Freud), Fall 2022

Three Ways to Be Wrong About Yourself

My first critical theory class was a semester of Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud, and I wrote four short papers for it. Reading them back after the colloquium, what stands out is that all three thinkers go after the same target — the confident, self-possessed person who understands their own economy, their own language, and their own mind — and each takes out a different support.

Marx first. My paper on commodity fetishism is about how something as ordinary as a table gets its strange power: once it becomes a commodity it stands “on its head” among other commodities, and we start attributing relations to the goods instead of to the people who produced them. The fetishist, I wrote, finds it more remarkable that wood can be abstracted into a commodity than he would if the table started dancing. The mistake isn't stupidity, it's structural: under capitalism, production is non-transparent, so of course we see prices and not labor. De-fetishizing means making production visible again, which for Marx ends in communism, the one mode of social production he thought was transparent.

Nietzsche goes after language and morality, and the two papers I wrote on him come at it from opposite ends. In On Truth and Lying in a Non-Moral Sense, our concepts are metaphors that have forgotten they're metaphors — the paper's summary of the overarching message is that “we only think we are in control,” and language is the vessel for that belief. Water has been chemically constant since the birth of the universe, but it's agua in Spanish and shuǐ in Mandarin; nothing in the molecule chose either word, and yet each feels like the truth of the thing. In The Gay Science, the death of God is the other half. Nietzsche's concern isn't the blasphemy but what comes after: the shadows of the divine stay on the cave wall long after the thing itself is gone. The end of authority isn't liberty — “an empty vessel simply cannot be filled with liberty” — and what fills the marketplace instead, in my paper's phrasing, is “monotonous consumption without meaning.”

Freud's target is the mind itself. In Civilization and Its Discontents, the ego — the thing we're surest of — turns out to be a shrunken remnant of an earlier ego that once contained the whole world, with the “oceanic feeling” as its leftover. Discontent isn't a malfunction, it's the price of growing up: every child learns the reality principle by losing something, first the mother who can't always be there, and spends the rest of their life budgeting pleasure against it. Freud calls happiness “an economic problem of libido,” and as I noted, the implication is that we should allocate our love like a scarce resource.

Put them next to each other and a pattern shows up: the economic actor, the speaking subject, and the rational self each turn out to be built on something they can't see — production, metaphor, the unconscious. Take all three seriously and you don't get a better humanism; you get something closer to the anti-humanist position I ended up defending in my senior colloquium, whether or not I had the word for it in 2022. What none of the three gives you is a clean answer to what to build instead. Marx has communism; Nietzsche, in my paper's words, has the promise that “there are better ways of living”; Freud has a household budget for the libido. Given my history with budgets, that last one was weirdly the most reassuring.

September 2026 · coursework: CSS 220 tutorial (welfare economics), Spring 2024

Every Welfare Function Has an Author

The CSS 220 tutorial was one question asked eight different ways: how would you know whether a society was better off? I wrote a paper a week on it in spring 2024. Reading all eight in a row now, what stands out is that I never got a clean answer, and neither did anyone I was assigned. Each school of economists believes it has finally escaped the value judgment. Each one has really just moved it somewhere harder to see.

The classical version is the most honest, because it picks something to count and says so. For Smith and Ricardo it was labor: a measure of value that survives the currency changing underneath it. By Pigou, in Week 4, the thing being counted is welfare itself, and the interesting move is his gap between private and social net product — the training an employer pays for that benefits everyone but shows up on nobody's books. He was described as having “a scientific head” outlining an “ethical heart,” and when asked what makes something good, he was said to answer, more or less, that what is good is good. That is a system with an ethics it declines to defend.

Then Robbins arrives and says you can't compare one person's satisfaction to another's at all. The standard argument for redistribution, that a dollar matters less to the rich, he dismisses as “merely specious,” an intuition dressed as a law. The rescue in my Week 5 paper is Kaldor and Hicks: a policy is fine if the winners could compensate the losers, whether or not anyone ever does. I defended that test at the time. I'd put it differently now. It's efficiency with the receipt removed, and it lets the economist keep saying “science” while somebody else quietly absorbs the loss.

Week 6 is where it gets uncomfortable, because the social welfare function makes the author explicit. Someone has to choose the weights. Bergson's point, as I understood it, is that the function doesn't care who: a benevolent tyrant, a megalomaniac, or a democratic assembly can all fill it in. A Rawlsian planner can reduce poverty and increase inequality at the same time, and to some governments that is the desired outcome. Baumol had already warned about the utilitarian shortcut: the same amount of money is likely to be a much greater loss to the poorer group than the corresponding gain to the richer. Every version of “society is better off” turns out to be a claim about whose losses count for how much.

Arrow and Coase, in Week 7, are the ones who tried hardest to keep the discipline clean, and they're the ones who make the case against it most cleanly. Arrow shows that you can't combine individual rankings into a social one without either imposing it or handing it to a dictator, and concedes that “there is no meaning to total output independent of distribution, i.e., of ethical judgements.” Coase's preference for marketable pollution permits over a Pigouvian tax is presented as description, but it is a prescription, and my paper's suspicion was that it reeks of energy-sector lobbying. My conclusion was that economics can't be separated from politics, and the line I'd still keep is that no one is a libertarian in a bank run.

Week 8 brings the argument home. Cass Sunstein's The Cost-Benefit Revolution holds that cost-benefit analysis has survived Reagan, Clinton, Bush, Obama, and even Trump, and reads that survival as proof that it's neutral. I read it as proof that they all came from the same school. The paper's thesis, which I'd still sign, is that CBA is only as good as those wielding it.

Here is the part I didn't have the material to write in 2024. The first data point on the deficit chart on my Projects page is Spring 2024, the same semester as these papers: a student activities fee fund running $200–300K short, which I helped cut by more than $100K over the following year and which was balanced by graduation. I didn't write a social welfare function to do it. But every version of the choice was one. When the fund is that far short, you can cut everyone a little or a few groups a lot. You can protect the events that draw the most students, or the ones with no other source of money. You can count what's easy to count, like attendance and cost per head, or argue for the things that don't show up in a survey. Each of those is a weighting, and I was the planner, or one of them.

This is also the equivalence principle from the Adorno entry above: whatever can't be measured gets declared an illusion, and the measurable thing gets optimized. I put turnout numbers on the Projects page because they're the numbers that exist. I would like to say I only did it because they're the honest ones. The Week 8 paper suggests the more accurate sentence is that they're the ones I know how to defend. The tutorial's whole conclusion was that a welfare measure is only as good as whoever holds it, which read as a line about Sunstein in 2024 and reads now as a line about me.
